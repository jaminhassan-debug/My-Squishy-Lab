Squishy Simulator Textures: Foam, Gel & Bouncy Jelly

What changes when you choose a squishy texture?

A squishy simulator turns touch or mouse input into changes in a virtual toy's shape. The material choice helps determine how the body moves while you press it and how it returns after you let go.

My Squishy Lab has three feels to explore, called Soft Foam, Dense Gel and Bouncy Jelly. You choose one during making, after the colour stage and before adding your inclusions.

Soft Foam and its slow rise

Soft Foam is the slowest to recover of the three choices. Press the sample, let go and watch the dent rise back towards its starting shape.

That slower return gives you more time to notice the outline changing after release. It is a useful material to try first if you want to learn the difference between the moment of pressing and the recovery that follows.

For a clearer comparison, make one foam toy with a simple colour. A body with fewer visual details lets you concentrate on the way the surface responds to a poke or squeeze.

Dense Gel and its soft wobble

Dense Gel sits between the slow rise of foam and the quicker spring of Bouncy Jelly. Its sample is described in the game as having a soft wobble.

Try giving it one deliberate press and then taking your finger away. Watching a single release makes the motion easier to follow than repeatedly pressing different places at once.

Gel is also an interesting choice when your design centres on the contents of the toy. You can move between Play and View to explore the body and look at the inclusions you selected while making it.

Bouncy Jelly and its quick spring

Bouncy Jelly recovers more quickly and has a springier release. Compare it directly with foam using the samples, keeping your presses as similar as you can.

If you have made a favourite shape in gel already, build a second version with Bouncy Jelly and keep the colour unchanged. That gives you two saved toys where the feel is the main variable.

The words soft, dense and bouncy describe the game's simulated behaviours. Your screen gives you visual and audio feedback, while the physical surface you touch is still the screen of your device.

A poke and a hand squeeze show different things

A poke affects a smaller area of the squishy. It is useful for seeing how a local dent forms and how the surrounding surface returns after release.

A hand squeeze changes a broader area of the body. Use it when you want to see how the overall outline responds, especially on round shapes such as a dumpling or blob.

Release between gestures while you are comparing materials. A consistent rhythm makes it easier to notice the differences in recovery instead of judging the toy while several movements overlap.

Stretching on a touch screen

Use two fingers to try stretching a toy on a compatible touch screen. Begin with a modest pull and release before trying a wider movement.

A tablet gives your fingers more room around the toy, while a phone offers a smaller area to work in. Landscape orientation helps keep the interaction space clear of the surrounding controls.

The View mode is useful for inspecting the toy without performing another squeeze. Switch back to Play when you want your next touch to interact with the body.

Compare materials with one simple experiment

Choose one mould and one colour, then make three versions using the different feels. Keep the inclusion recipe consistent, or leave it simple, so it is easier to compare the movement.

Save each version and revisit them from the shelf. Start with a poke, continue with a hand squeeze and finish with a two finger stretch if your device supports it.

The experiment gives you a reference collection for future designs. When a new idea calls for a slower rise or a quicker spring, you can choose from something you have already tried.

Adjust the sound to suit your session

The Grown-ups audio settings include controls for music, sound effects and voice. Quiet mode is another option when you want to change how the session feels.

Try the same material with effects on and off to decide which you prefer. The visible shape change remains useful feedback while you compare your toys.
